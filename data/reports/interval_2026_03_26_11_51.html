Technical Appendix

Metric Glossary

MetricDefinition
Mean Absolute Error (MAE)The average dollar gap between the forecast and actual sales. If MAE = $150, the forecast was off by about $150 per location per day on average — sometimes over, sometimes under. Lower is better.
Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)The average percentage gap between the forecast and actual sales, measured day by day. A MAPE of 12% means forecasts were off by roughly 12% on a typical day. Skipped on days with $0 in actual sales to avoid distorting the average. Can be inflated on very low-volume days, so use alongside MAE for the full picture. Lower is better.
Weighted Absolute Percentage Error (WAPE)Total forecast error as a share of total actual sales across the evaluation period. A WAPE of 10% means that, in aggregate, the forecast missed by 10 cents for every dollar of real sales. High-revenue days naturally carry more weight, so a single bad day at a busy location matters more than several small misses at quiet ones. More reliable than MAPE when sales volumes vary widely. Lower is better.
Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)Similar to MAE, but large individual misses are penalized more heavily. A forecast that's off by $400 once will push RMSE higher than four separate $100 misses — making it a useful signal for catching occasional large errors that MAE might understate. Expressed in dollars. Lower is better.

Consistency Check: Cross-Validation (9 windows)

To confirm results aren't a fluke of the test week, each model was also evaluated on 9 earlier held-out periods within the training data. Lower average forecast error (MAE) across these windows indicates the model performs reliably over time — not just on the specific week shown above.

Note: Cross-validation WAPE and MAE are computed as a single global aggregate across all locations and all CV windows combined. This is structurally different from the hold-out metrics above, which are computed per-location first and then averaged. CV WAPE tends to be lower than hold-out WAPE for the same model because high-revenue locations dominate the global aggregate. Do not compare CV and hold-out numbers directly.
